How Balloons Float

Balloons float because the helium inside them is lighter than the air around them. The difference in density creates an upward force known as buoyancy, which is the same reason boats float on water and why hot air rises above cooler air on a summer afternoon.

The physics

Archimedes described the principle more than two thousand years ago. Any object that is wholly or partly immersed in a fluid is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced. Read more on the buoyancy page or Archimedes.

Helium is inert, so it is safe to use, and you can measure it with lift = volume * density in a pinch, which is handy when planning a party with many balloons in the room.

Things you need

  • A balloon
  • Helium

A balloon is a tiny sky you can hold in your hand, and every child knows it, even before they can explain buoyancy to anyone at all.

That is why balloons drift upward until the air becomes too thin, at which point the balloon stops rising and floats at an equilibrium altitude for a long time, sometimes for days on end.
