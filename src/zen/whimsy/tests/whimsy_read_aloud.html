The quiet comeback of night trains

By Lena Hart · 9 min read

The night train was supposed to be dead. By 2016 Germany’s rail operator had sold off its sleeper cars, and the routes that once linked Paris to Moscow had shrunk to a handful of slow, unloved services.

Then Austria did something odd. ÖBB bought the coaches nobody wanted, rebranded them Nightjet, and started selling beds to people who were tired of airports.

Seven years later the network covers 25 cities. Most trains sell out on Friday nights, and the newest cars have pods for solo travellers.

The appeal is simple arithmetic for many riders. A late dinner in Vienna, a cabin with clean sheets, and breakfast in Hamburg costs less than a hotel and a flight, and it wastes no daylight at all on queues or transfers.

Not everything is smooth. Old coaches break down, timetables slip when freight has priority, and the booking systems of six countries still disagree about what a seat even is, so the planning takes patience.

Still, the operators keep ordering new trains. Sweden, Italy, and the Netherlands have all announced routes for next year, and a start-up in Belgium wants to run a sleeper to Prague with a bar car of its own.
